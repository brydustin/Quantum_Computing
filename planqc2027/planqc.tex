\titlenote{This extended abstract summarizes a 37-page paper in preparation, available from the author.
The Isabelle sources are at \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23812498}, and each logo~\isalogo{} links to a proof.}
\author{Dustin Bryant}

\begin{abstract}
The HHL algorithm of Harrow, Hassidim and Lloyd solves a linear system $Ax=b$ on a quantum
computer, in the sense that it prepares a quantum state whose amplitudes are proportional to $x$.
Earlier machine-checked proofs cover only the idealised case, in which every eigenvalue of $A$ is
exactly representable in the algorithm's finite precision, and they treat its subroutines as black
boxes. We verify HHL in Isabelle/HOL, from the spectral theorem through quantum Hoare logic down to
circuits of elementary one- and two-qubit gates. Four small proof rules make the logic practical for
a whole algorithm. In the idealised case, the program that repeats the circuit until a measurement
signals success is totally correct, and its number of rounds is geometric with mean $1/p$, where $p$
is the probability that a round succeeds. Beyond that case, the original paper's error analysis
holds with explicit constants, and for a concrete family of sparse matrices the whole algorithm,
including the subroutines that access the matrix, becomes a verified list of gates whose number is
bounded by an explicit formula. Along the way we found seven false statements in the original paper.
An AI agent wrote almost all of the 64{,}000 lines of Isabelle, directed by the author.
\end{abstract}

\section{HHL, and Why It Is Hard to Verify}

HHL~\cite{hhl2009} started quantum linear algebra. Given a sparse Hermitian $n\times n$ matrix $A$
and a vector $b$ stored as a quantum state $\ket b$, it prepares the state $\ket x\propto A^{-1}\ket b$
in time polynomial in $\log n$, the condition number $\kappa$ and the inverse precision
$1/\varepsilon$. It works in four steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:hhl}). Phase estimation, a standard quantum
subroutine, writes the eigenvalues of $A$ into a register of $k$ qubits that we call the clock; a
rotation controlled by the clock moves the amplitude $C/\lambda$ into one extra qubit, the ancilla;
the clock is uncomputed, that is, reset by running phase estimation backwards; and the ancilla is
measured. On outcome~$1$ the register that held $b$ holds $A^{-1}b$ up to normalisation. This happens
with probability $p=C^2\norm{A^{-1}b}^2$, so the run is repeated until it succeeds, which makes HHL a
\emph{repeat-until-success} program. From a programming-languages perspective, HHL is a probabilistic
program, a loop guarded by a measurement, whose correctness is a statement about the distribution of
its outputs and of its running time.

HHL is hard to verify along every axis at once. Its specification is probabilistic, and the number
of rounds is random. Every step is approximate, since eigenvalues are rarely representable exactly with $k$
clock qubits, and the evolution $e^{iAt}$ must itself be approximated by gates (Hamiltonian
simulation). Its black boxes, the preparation of $\ket b$ and the controlled
evolution, are where the cost is. The mechanised proofs we know of, in Coq~\cite{zhou2023coqq,wu2024local}, verify
the idealised \emph{exact case}, in which every eigenvalue is representable, and take the black boxes
as given unitaries (the reversible operations of quantum computing); CoqQ proves partial correctness of the repeat-until-success program~\cite{zhou2023coqq},
following the pen-and-paper proof of Zhou, Yu and Ying~\cite{zhou2019applied}. In Lean,
QEDIA~\cite{ren2026qedia} proves a quantum Hoare logic sound for total correctness, with phase
estimation, repeat-until-success circuits and Hamiltonian simulation as case studies, but not HHL.

\paragraph{Contributions} We work in Isabelle/HOL, on QHLProver's formalisation~\cite{liu2019qhl}
of Ying's quantum while-language~\cite{ying2011}, and contribute the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Total correctness.} The repeat-until-success program terminates with probability one in
  the normalised solution, exits after exactly $\ell$ failed rounds with probability $(1-p)^\ell p$, and runs
  $1/p$ rounds on average (Section~\ref{sec:qhl}).\hfill\isalink{Algorithms/HHL_Termination.thy}{463}
\item \emph{The approximations.} The paper's Theorem~1, its error bound beyond the exact case, holds
  with the constant $c=\pi^2\sqrt{4+2\pi^2}$, and so does its run-time analysis, where amplitude
  amplification cuts the rounds from $1/p$ to about $1/\sqrt p$.\hfill\isalink{Algorithms/HHL_Filter_Bounds.thy}{154}
\item \emph{The subroutines.} Phase estimation, as a matrix identity\isalink{Algorithms/Phase_Estimation.thy}{287} and as a program\isalink{Algorithms/QPE_Program.thy}{177}; amplitude
  amplification; Hamiltonian simulation by Trotter products, from black boxes (oracles) that list the
  nonzero entries of a sparse matrix; preparation of $\ket b$; and the clock-controlled rotation; the
  quantum Fourier transform is the AFP's~\cite{manrique2025qft}.
\item \emph{The gates.} For the shifted Laplacians of cycle graphs, a concrete family of sparse
  matrices, the whole algorithm is a list of one- and two-qubit gates whose number has an explicit
  bound
  (Section~\ref{sec:gates}).\hfill\isalink{Algorithms/Gates/HHL_Laplacian_Sparse.thy}{77}
\item \emph{The errata.} Seven statements of the HHL paper are false (Section~\ref{sec:errata}).
\item \emph{The process.} An AI agent wrote almost all of the 130 theories, with no \texttt{sorry} (Section~\ref{sec:agent}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Quantum Hoare Logic at Algorithm Scale}\label{sec:qhl}

Programs of the while-language denote completely positive, trace-non-increasing maps $\sem S$ on
partial density operators, the quantum analogue of subprobability distributions. A predicate is an operator $0\le P\le I$, and $\hoaret{P}{S}{Q}$ holds if
$\Tr(P\rho)\le\Tr(Q\,\sem S\rho)$ for every state $\rho$; so $\hoaret{p\cdot I}{S}{Q}$ says that $S$
ends in $Q$ with probability at least $p$.

Four rules carry all of our program proofs. Three are derived in Ying's system, namely \emph{initialisation},
\emph{gate lists}, and their composition for the shape \emph{initialise, apply gates, measure} shared by
phase estimation, one run of HHL and all its inexact variants.\isalink{Algorithms/HHL_Program.thy}{378} With $W$ the product of the gates and
$\psi=W\ket0$ the state they prepare,
\begin{gather*}
\{\bra0Q\ket0\cdot I\}\;\mathit{init}\;\{Q\},\qquad\{W^\dagger QW\}\;U_1;\dots;U_m\;\{Q\},\\
\Bigl\{\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_j\langle M_j\psi|\,Q\,|M_j\psi\rangle\Bigr)\cdot I\Bigr\}\;
\mathit{init};\,U_1;\dots;U_m;\,\mathbf{measure}\ M\;\{Q\}.
\end{gather*}
None is deep, but together they turn every probabilistic claim about HHL into one computation. For
$Q=\ketbra tt$ with $\ket t$ in the success branch, the scalar is the fidelity $|\langle t|\psi\rangle|^2$, the probability of ending in $\ket t$,
so every error bound of the analysis becomes a triple. The fourth rule, for \emph{resetting loops}, whose body starts by re-initialising the state, is
proved from the semantics, because QHLProver formalises partial correctness only and Ying's
total-correctness rule needs a bound function~\cite{ying2011}.\hfill\isalink{Algorithms/HHL_Termination.thy}{210}

\paragraph{The HHL loop} The repeat-until-success program
\[
\mathit{hhl\_rus}\;=\;\mathit{init};\,W;\;\mathbf{while}\ \mathit{ancilla}=0\ \mathbf{do}\ (\mathit{init};\,W)
\]
runs the eight gates $W=U_8\cdots U_1$ of Fig.~\ref{fig:hhl} until the ancilla reads~$1$. Because the body begins with $\mathit{init}$, it maps every input $\rho$ to $(\Tr\rho)\,\ketbra\psi\psi$ for
the one state $\psi=W\ket0=\ket0\otimes w$. A round succeeds with probability
$p=\norm{P_1\psi}^2=C^2\norm{A^{-1}b}^2$ and then leaves $\ketbra tt$; otherwise the next round starts
afresh. The loop is the geometric series $\sum_\ell(1-p)^\ell p\,\ketbra tt=\ketbra tt$, and one lemma about
such loops gives termination, the exit distribution and the mean number of rounds. AutoQ checks
two-qubit repeat-until-success circuits automatically and found bugs in published
ones~\cite{chen2026autoq}; our loop body is a whole algorithm.

\begin{theorem}[exact HHL, repeat until success]
Let $A$ be Hermitian with every eigenvalue on the grid $2\pi j/(Nt_0)$, $-N/2\le j<N/2$, $j\ne0$, of a clock
with $N=2^k$ values, let $U_b\ket0=b$, and let $0<|C|\le2\pi/(Nt_0)$. Then
$\hoaret{I}{\mathit{hhl\_rus}}{\ketbra tt}$ for $\ket t=\ket0\otimes A^{-1}b/\norm{A^{-1}b}\otimes\ket1$,
the loop exits after exactly $\ell$ failed rounds with probability $(1-p)^\ell p$, and it runs $1/p$ rounds on
average.\hfill\isalink{Algorithms/HHL_Termination.thy}{463}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The hypotheses are the specification} Listing~\ref{lst:rus}, with the definitions it
names, is all that a reader has to trust. Measured as in Lean-QuantumAlg~\cite{zhang2026leanquantumalg}, that is 71 lines, 0.35\% of the 20{,}120 lines of our theories that it
imports, with Isabelle/HOL and the AFP audited once (360 lines, 1.06\%, for the gate-level theorem). Writing it down exposed a restriction that informal accounts
hide. The bound on $C$ comes from the program, not from the mathematics. The circuit applies
$R(C/\lambda_j)$ on every clock value, and a gate must be unitary. The bound costs nothing when the
smallest modulus $\lambda_{\min}$ of an eigenvalue is the first grid point $\lambda_1=2\pi/(Nt_0)$, and
otherwise lowers the best possible $p$ by the factor $(\lambda_1/\lambda_{\min})^2$.

\section{From Black Boxes to Gates}\label{sec:gates}

The programs above take $U_b$, the controlled evolution and the controlled rotation as unitaries. We
refine them until every gate is \emph{elementary}, that is, a one- or two-qubit unitary on adjacent wires.
Every program theorem is stated for all gate lists with a given product, so refinement never reopens
it. For $s$-sparse
$A$, with at most $s$ nonzero entries per row, all bounded by $L$, whose graph is coloured into $2s-1$
matchings given by oracles, $r$
Trotter steps, each applying the evolution of every matching once, are within $8(sLt)^2/r$ of $e^{iAt}$ and make $r(6s-2)$ oracle queries.\hfill\isalink{Algorithms/HHL_Sparse_Circuit.thy}{915}

For the Laplacian of the cycle on $2^m$ vertices, shifted by $\mu\ge0$, the oracles themselves are
compiled, as reversible arithmetic routed to adjacent wires, and so is the preparation of a sparse
nonnegative $b$. One theorem collects the result.\isalink{Algorithms/Gates/HHL_Laplacian_Sparse.thy}{77} It gives an explicit list of elementary gates on
$K=m+k+1+a$ qubits whose output, when the ancilla reads~$1$, is within $2\delta/(C\norm x)$ of $x/\norm x$,
where $x$ solves the system for the right-hand side that the gates actually prepare. The error budget
$\delta=\epsilon+2(2^k-1)\eta$ adds the error $\epsilon$ of phase estimation to an error $\eta$ for each
simulated evolution, and the length is an explicit polynomial, $O(4^k(2+\mu)^2t_0\,m^2K/C)$, which is quadratic
in the clock size because first-order Trotter steps must shrink as the clock grows. Black boxes hide the cost. The
algorithm and its programs take 19 theories, and the oracles of this one family take 39 more. Like the
end-to-end proof of
Shor's algorithm in SQIR~\cite{peng2023shor}, the pipeline leaves nothing abstract; unlike verified
oracle compilers~\cite{amy2017reverc,li2022vqo}, it compiles the oracles of one family only.

\section{Seven Errata}\label{sec:errata}

Proving the paper's statements as written, appendix included, refuted seven of them.
Table~\ref{tab:errata} lists each with its counter-example and its correction, both proved in
Isabelle. The most instructive is Lemma~3, the Lipschitz estimate behind
Theorem~1, which fails for every $\kappa$. Its constant must be $\pi^2$, not $\pi^2/2$, and $\pi^2$ is sharp\isalink{Algorithms/HHL_Filter.thy}{567}.
None of the errors invalidates a main claim. Most sit where a sketch compresses a computation, such as an
endpoint value, an index, or a number of rounds or samples. A reader checks the argument; a proof
assistant checks the arithmetic, where the sketch was wrong.

\section{How It Was Built}\label{sec:agent}

AI agents, Claude Code~\cite{claudecode} with Claude Opus~4.7, Fable~5.1 and Opus~5.5 and Codex with
GPT-5.4, wrote the development under the author's direction and drafted this abstract; the author takes
full responsibility for its content. The foundation, Paulsen's lecture
course~\cite{paulsen2011notes}, was formalised in April and May 2026. After a pause of 147 days, HHL
followed in eleven days of October, with 258 messages from the author over 84 distinct hours and
9{,}835 tool calls by the agent. The agent wrote and repaired the proofs, and after the pause it
picked up the work from its own hand-off note. The author chose what to prove, checked that each
statement meant what the paper meant, and asked on 28 October for the black boxes to be built from
gates. The kernel rejects wrong proofs, so the agent's mistakes lay elsewhere. Once it confidently
reported ``no oracles'' from an audit that had skipped the gate theories, where two theorems depended
on \texttt{skip\_proof}. Checks in the build now catch such mistakes. The kernel guarantees that the
theorems are true, and the author that they are the right theorems.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}

We would value the community's view on four questions. Should closed forms like ours be built into
tools for quantum Hoare logic? Could verified gate counts like ours back the cost models of languages
for quantum linear algebra, such as Cobble~\cite{yuan2026cobble}? What should
``verified to the gate'' mean once the gate set is finite, as in Clifford+T~\cite{ross2016gridsynth}, and the oracles general? And as agents write
more of our proofs, which checks should a verified quantum library require? Our next steps are clamping the rotation, higher-order product
formulas, and a second prover.
